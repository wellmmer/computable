In 1936, Alan Turing published a paper called *On Computable Numbers*.
It describes a machine that nobody built.
Today we call it the Turing machine.
This lesson shows where the idea came from.
It starts with a person, a pencil and a sheet of squared paper.

## A question about methods

Turing wanted to answer a question from mathematics.
The question asked if one fixed method can decide, for any formula of logic, whether it can be proved.
Its German name is the *Entscheidungsproblem*, the "decision problem".
A later lesson tells that story.

To answer the question, Turing first needed a clear idea of a method.
People had used methods for a long time, such as long division.
But nobody had said exactly what counts as a method.
Turing found a way to say it.
He looked closely at a person who follows a method.

## The human computer

In 1936, the word "computer" meant a person who computes.
Turing pictured such a person at work.
His analysis starts with these words:

> “Computing is normally done by writing certain symbols on paper. We may suppose this paper is divided into squares like a child's arithmetic book.” (Turing, 1936, page 249)

Think of a student who adds two long numbers on squared paper.
The student writes one digit in each square.
The student looks at a few squares, writes a digit, and moves on.
At each moment, the student also keeps something in mind, such as "I carry a 1".

Turing called this a "state of mind".
He wrote:

> “The behaviour of the computer at any moment is determined by the symbols which he is observing, and his ‘state of mind’ at that moment.” (Turing, 1936, page 250)

So two things decide the next action.
One is what the person sees on the paper.
The other is what the person has in mind.

## Turing makes the picture simpler

Next, Turing took away every detail that a method does not need.
Each point below drops one detail and keeps the method.

- **One row of squares.** A sheet of paper has rows and columns. Turing argued that one long row is enough. Work on a sheet can always be written out in one row.
- **A fixed set of symbols.** The person uses a limited set of symbols, such as the digits 0 to 9. A long number is just several symbols side by side.
- **A few squares at a time.** A person can look at only a few squares at once. To see more, the person must look again, one glance after another.
- **A fixed set of states of mind.** A person can keep only a limited number of things in mind. Anything more can be written down on the paper instead.
- **Very small steps.** Each step changes at most one symbol. Each step moves the person's attention only a short way.

Turing argued that these limits do not weaken the method.
A person who follows them can still do the same work.
The work may only take more squares and more steps.

## From the person to the machine

Now put a machine in the place of the person.
Each part of the picture becomes one part of the machine.

- The row of squares becomes the **tape**. The tape is divided into cells, and each cell holds one symbol.
- The square the person looks at becomes the cell under the **head**. The head reads that one cell, and it can write a new symbol there.
- The state of mind becomes the machine's **state**. Turing called it an "m-configuration".
- The method becomes a table of **rules**. Each rule says: in this state, reading this symbol, do these things, then go to that state.

The head moves only one cell at a time, to the left or to the right.
That is enough, because the machine can repeat a move as often as it needs.
The tape has no end, so the machine never runs out of cells.

The machine has no other memory.
It cannot look at the whole tape at once.
The machines in this lesson, and in this simulator, never choose between rules.
For each state and symbol, there is at most one rule.
In this simulator, a machine stops when no rule fits.

## Turing's first machine

Turing's paper soon gives a first example.
This machine prints 0 1 0 1 … on the tape, without end.
It starts on a blank tape, in the state 𝔟.
Turing named his states with old German letters: 𝔟, 𝔠, 𝔢 and 𝔨.

The machine has four rules, and each one reads a blank cell:

- In 𝔟, it prints 0, moves right, and goes to 𝔠.
- In 𝔠, it moves right and goes to 𝔢.
- In 𝔢, it prints 1, moves right, and goes to 𝔨.
- In 𝔨, it moves right and goes to 𝔟.

Press **Step** four times.
Watch the state above the head change from 𝔟 to 𝔠, to 𝔢, to 𝔨, and back to 𝔟.

The machine leaves a blank cell after each digit.
In his paper, Turing keeps every other cell free for rough notes.
This machine needs no notes, so those cells stay blank.

The machine never stops.
After 𝔨, it always goes back to 𝔟.
For Turing, this was not a problem.
He wanted machines that write the digits of a number, one after another, without end.

## A machine that adds one

Turing wrote his rules in his own notation.
Most textbooks today use a shorter form.
Each rule reads one symbol, writes one symbol, and names the next state.
It also moves the head one cell left or right, or leaves the head where it is.
The simulator in Computable uses this form too.

The next machine adds 1 to a number written in binary.
The input 1011 is the number eleven.
The machine should end with 1100, the number twelve.

It works like a person who adds 1 by hand:

1. In state q0, the head moves right until it reads the blank after the last digit.
2. Then it steps back onto the last digit and goes to state q1.
3. In q1, a 1 becomes 0, and the head moves left. This is the carry.
4. In q1, a 0 or a blank becomes 1. The work is done, and the machine goes to qA, its accept state.

Press **Run** and watch the carry move to the left.

The machine stops in qA after 8 steps.
The tape now reads 1100.
Open it in the simulator to see all six rules, or to try another input.

## Why this matters

Turing's machine is very simple.
It reads, writes and moves, one cell at a time.
Yet Turing argued that it can do any computation that a person can do by following fixed rules.
Today this claim is called the Church–Turing thesis.
A later lesson looks at it closely.

The machine also gave Turing his answer.
With a clear idea of a method, he could show that some questions have no method at all.
Later lessons explain how.
