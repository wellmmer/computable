Lesson 5 ended with a gap.
Turing proved that no machine can decide, for every formula, whether it is provable.
But Hilbert and Ackermann asked about any method, not only about machines.
This lesson is about the claim that closes this gap.
It follows section 9 and the appendix of Turing's 1936 paper.

## The claim

Lesson 1 showed how Turing pictured a person who computes with a pencil.
He took away every detail that a method does not need.
What was left was a machine with a tape, a head, states and rules.

From this picture comes a claim:

**What a person can compute by following a fixed method, a Turing machine can compute.**

A fixed method is a set of rules that say exactly what to do at each step.
The person needs no new ideas, only care and time.
Long division is such a method.
The person may use as much paper and time as the method needs.

Today this claim is called the Church–Turing thesis.

## Why it is a thesis and not a theorem

A theorem is a claim with a proof.
A proof needs an exact definition for every idea in the claim.
"Turing machine" has an exact definition: lesson 2 gave it.
"A fixed method that a person can follow" has none.
It describes something that people do.
So no proof can link the two sides.

Turing said so himself, at the start of section 9:

> “No attempt has yet been made to show that the ‘computable’ numbers include all numbers which would naturally be regarded as computable. All arguments which can be given are bound to be, fundamentally, appeals to intuition, and for this reason rather unsatisfactory mathematically.” (Turing, 1936, page 249)

Instead of a proof, Turing gives arguments of three kinds (page 249):

- **A direct appeal to intuition.** This is the person with a pencil, from lesson 1.
- **A proof that two definitions are the same.** This helps when one of the definitions seems more natural.
- **Examples.** Turing shows large classes of numbers that machines can compute.

A thesis can still be wrong.
If someone found a fixed method that a person can follow, but that no Turing machine can carry out, the thesis would be false.

## What Turing's machines compute

Turing's machines print the figures 0 and 1.
The figures that a machine prints, in order, form the sequence it computes (page 232).
A sequence is computable if a circle-free machine computes it (page 233).
For example, Turing's first machine prints 0 1 0 1 …, so the sequence 010101… is computable.
Turing says that he will speak of sequences more often than of numbers (page 233).

## Church's idea

Alonzo Church worked at Princeton University.
He gave his own definition of what is "effectively calculable".
It has no tape and no head.
It works with formulas, written with the Greek letter λ (lambda).
Fixed rules turn one formula into another, one step at a time.
Turing calls these steps "conversion" (page 263).

A sequence is λ-definable if one formula gives every figure of it (page 263).
Apply the formula to the number n, and convert.
The result names the n-th figure of the sequence.

Turing saw that Church's idea and his own describe the same thing:

> “In a recent paper Alonzo Church has introduced an idea of ‘effective calculability’, which is equivalent to my ‘computability’, but is very differently defined.” (Turing, 1936, page 231)

## Turing's appendix

Turing proves this claim in an appendix at the end of his paper:

> “The theorem that all effectively calculable (λ-definable) sequences are computable and its converse are proved below in outline.” (Turing, 1936, page 263)

The appendix has two parts:

- **From Church to Turing.** Turing describes a machine that takes Church's formula for a sequence. The machine converts the formula, step by step, and prints the figures of the sequence (pages 263–264). So every λ-definable sequence is computable.
- **From Turing to Church.** For any circle-free machine, Turing shows how to find a formula that gives the same sequence (pages 264–265). So every computable sequence is λ-definable. This is the "converse".

Together, the two parts show that both definitions give exactly the same sequences.
Three details matter here:

- Turing speaks of sequences, not functions.
- He gives the proof "in outline". He skips some steps, and says so.
- The journal received the paper on 28 May 1936 (page 230). The appendix is marked "Added 28 August, 1936" (page 263).

The appendix proves that two definitions are the same.
That is the second kind of argument of section 9.
It supports the thesis, but it does not prove it.
Both definitions are exact.
"A fixed method that a person can follow" still is not.

## Why this matters

Now the gap of lesson 5 closes.
In section 9, Turing wrote:

> “Once it is granted that computable numbers are all ‘computable’, several other propositions of the same character follow. In particular, it follows that, if there is a general process for determining whether a formula of the Hilbert function calculus is provable, then the determination can be carried out by a machine.” (Turing, 1936, page 249)

So, if the thesis holds, every fixed method can be carried out by a machine.
Lesson 5 showed that no machine can decide, for every formula, whether it is provable.
So no fixed method can decide it either.

The thesis also explains why one simple machine matters so much.
If it holds, the machines in this simulator can do every computation that a person can do by a fixed method.
They only need enough tape and enough time.
